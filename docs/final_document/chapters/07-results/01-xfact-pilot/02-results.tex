\subsection{Results}
\label{subsec:pilot-results}

Every claim of both sets was scored: claims whose search or model was
never reached were re-run until none was left set apart.
Table~\ref{tab:pilot-results} gives accuracy and macro-averaged $F_1$
with percentile 95\% bootstrap intervals over claims (10,000 resamples,
seed 2026); macro-$F_1$ averages over the five verdicts the gold labels
contain, three for English, whose PolitiFact scale has neither
\texttt{MISLEADING} nor \texttt{UNVERIFIED}.

On the pilot the system is barely distinguishable from chance: Cohen's
$\kappa$ against the gold labels is 0.07, with an interval
($-0.03$ to $0.18$) that includes zero, and its macro-$F_1$ is below
what a uniform random guess is expected to score on a set balanced by
construction. The reason is visible in the confusion matrix: the
system answered \texttt{FALSE} to 43 of the 64 claims, only 14 of them
correctly (16 of the 64 are false), and never answered
\texttt{MISLEADING}. Asked without evidence,
the same model answers \texttt{FALSE} to 60 of 64, so this lean is the
model's prior showing through the evidence rather than a property of
the evidence. The evidence nevertheless helps: paired over the same
claims, the pipeline beats the closed-book model by 10.9 accuracy
points (interval 3.1 to 20.3) and 10.4 macro-$F_1$ points (4.5 to
16.8). On the Spanish test split the picture is worse: an accuracy of 19.4\%, below the 30.3\% of always answering the majority class, a $\kappa$ of $-0.002$ ($-0.06$ to $0.06$), \texttt{FALSE} for 107 of the 165 claims (24 are false) and \texttt{MISLEADING} never, for the second-largest class; and there the evidence buys nothing measurable over the closed-book model, which answers \texttt{FALSE} to 155 of 164 claims (3.0 accuracy points, interval $-3.0$ to $9.1$).

\paragraph{Retrieval.} For the 38 Spanish claims with reference links
(the English rows carry none), the system retrieved none of the
annotator's links at any depth, and a reference \emph{domain} among its
candidates for 10.5\% of claims. It found evidence for 98.4\% of the
claims, about 18 candidates and 4.7 ranked sources each. Under the
trace-only rule of Section~\ref{sec:harness}, 44 of the 45 wrong
verdicts are reasoning errors; under the reference rule, 27 of the 29
it can judge are retrieval errors. The two rules disagree on 28 of
those 29: the model misread what it was shown, and what it was shown
was not what the fact-checker had used.

\paragraph{Cost.} A claim took 36.5~s at the median (90th percentile
59.9~s): 14.8~s of retrieval, 8.6~s of ranking and a 5.6~s model call
of about 2,100 prompt and 180 completion tokens. At two claims at once,
the 64 claims take about 18 minutes on the development laptop.

\paragraph{What the pilot found in the tooling.} The harness was run
against live services here for the first time. It recorded each
resumed session's timeouts and concurrency only once, for the first
session, which was fixed and is now tested; inference timed out under
four concurrent claims on the CPU, and the run was resumed with a
longer timeout and two claims at once; a model caught in a repetition
loop was recorded as unreachable rather than as a model failure; the
check of the working tree that stamps each record with its commit
outlasted its timeout over the container's mount and lost one
session's commit, which was fixed; and the harness process crashed
twice in native code while parsing scraped pages. An interruption of
the container runtime and both crashes were absorbed by the resume
rule, which re-ran exactly the claims each had lost.
