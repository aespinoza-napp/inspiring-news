\section{Existing Tools and Services}
\label{sec:sota-tools}

\paragraph{Fact-checking organisations.} Professional fact-checkers
publish verdicts on claims, mostly political, under their own rating
scales \cite{graves2016rise}. PolitiFact in the United States and
Chequeado in Argentina are the sources of the English and Spanish
claims of the X-Fact pilot (Section~\ref{sec:results-pilot}); in Spain,
Newtral and Maldita play the same role. Their articles are evidence of
the highest value for a claim they have checked, and this system rates
both Spanish organisations as evidence sources. It does not ingest
them as news: a fact-check quotes the claim it debunks, and claim
extraction would take that quotation for the article's own assertion
(Section~\ref{subsec:scraping-sources}).

\paragraph{Automated tools.} The research tools closest to practice
assist a step of the fact-checker's work: ClaimBuster scores sentences
for check-worthiness \cite{hassan2017claimbuster}, and the systems
surveyed by Nakov and colleagues find claims already checked, retrieve
evidence or flag claims for review \cite{nakov2021assisting}. General
assistants built on large language models answer questions with web
search and citations, but they are not designed to abstain, to refuse
a source they consider off-topic, or to report a failed search as a
failure; their verdicts and their confidence cannot be audited in the
way this work requires.

\paragraph{Constructive news services.} Outlets such as Good News
Network, Positive News and Reasons to be Cheerful, all in this system's
source catalogue, curate constructive stories editorially. Their
selection is human and their verification is the ordinary editorial
process of each outlet; neither is automated or exposed to the reader
claim by claim.

No existing tool combines the three steps this work joins: finding
constructive news on chosen topics, choosing what to analyse, and
verifying its claims with a verdict that shows its evidence.
